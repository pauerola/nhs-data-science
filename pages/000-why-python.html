Why Python?

What is Python?

Python is a general-purpose programming language first released in 1991. It was designed around a simple philosophy: code should be easy to read, easy to write, and easy to share. Python runs line by line in an interactive environment — which is why it works so naturally inside Jupyter Notebooks, where you can run a single cell, inspect the output, and decide what to do next. Python is free, open-source, and runs on any operating system.

Over the past decade, Python has become the dominant language for data science and machine learning. Its ecosystem — pandas for data wrangling, scikit-learn for machine learning, matplotlib and seaborn for visualisation, PyTorch and TensorFlow for deep learning — is unmatched in breadth and quality.

Why not R, Stata, or SAS?

Healthcare professionals approaching data science for the first time often encounter a confusing landscape of tools. R, Stata, SAS, and SPSS all claim to solve similar problems, and each has a legitimate place in the clinical research toolkit.

Python by the numbers

The evidence for Python’s dominance is unambiguous. After more than a decade of steady growth, Python’s adoption accelerated significantly in 2025, seeing a seven percentage point increase from 2024 — driven by its role as the go-to language for AI, data science, and back-end development, according to the Stack Overflow Developer Survey, the largest annual survey of the global developer community. Python is the most desired language among developers who are not currently using it but want to in the coming year, overtaking JavaScript — a striking signal of where the profession is heading. Among those learning to code, Python is the most popular language of all, reflecting how it has become the default entry point into programming for a new generation of scientists, engineers, and clinicians.

Applied health research fellowships at Oxford, Imperial, and UCL now routinely specify Python fluency for machine learning work, reflecting an expectation that serious health data scientists are anchored in Python even when they also use R for statistical modelling.

Python and the NHS digital transformation

For NHS doctors specifically, the argument is even more pointed. The NHS is in the middle of a substantial digital transformation, and Python has become the de facto language of NHS England’s data science function.

With the legal merger of NHS Digital into NHS England, the new NHS England became one of the largest digital and technology organisations in the country, directly operating services that patients and staff rely on every day. The 2025 Medium Term Planning Framework sets out that by 2028/29 all acute, community and mental health providers will be using the Federated Data Platform (FDP) to support elective recovery, cancer, and urgent and emergency care. As of May 2025, 91 per cent of secondary care trusts have an electronic patient record system, generating structured clinical data at a scale that requires programmatic analysis — not manual spreadsheets. NHS England publishes its own data science work as open Python code on GitHub, and the NHS England Data Science team explicitly positions Python as its primary language for analytical and machine learning work.

Crucially, NHS England has recognised that clinical staff — not just specialist data scientists — need these skills. The NHS Clinical Data Science Programme, funded by NHS England since 2023 with growing cohorts in 2024 and 2025, aims to empower healthcare professionals from across the health and social care workforce — from nurses and AHPs to doctors and healthcare scientists — to apply data science in practice and translate data into patient benefit.

Important points

NHS England publishes its data science code openly at github.com/NHSEngland. Browsing those repositories gives a realistic sense of how Python is used in NHS analytical work — from population health models to waiting list analytics. Almost everything is Python.

What this means for you

The skills you build in this course — pandas for data wrangling, scikit-learn for machine learning, seaborn for visualisation — are exactly the skills listed in NHS data science job specifications, clinical informatics fellowships, and applied health research postdoctoral positions. They are also the foundation for everything that comes next: deep learning, natural language processing of clinical notes, survival analysis, and Bayesian statistical modelling.

That is why we start here.
